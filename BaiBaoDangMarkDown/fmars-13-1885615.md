# Improved Transformer-based detection of underwater plastic debris in complex environments 

Yiyang Li<sup>1</sup> , Mengsong Luan<sup>1</sup> , Hanpeng Yu<sup>1</sup> and Huayun Yu<sup>1,2*</sup> 

> 1School of Computer Science, Yangtze University, Jingzhou, Hubei, China, 2Artificial Intelligence Research Platform, Yangtze University, Jingzhou, Hubei, China 

Introduction: Underwater plastic debris detection remains challenging in visually cluttered environments because underwater images are often severely degraded, debris instances are small, and backgrounds are complex. To address these challenges, this study develops an improved RF-DETR detector for underwater plastic debris detection. 

Methods: The proposed architecture integrates an underwater frequency-aware feature reweighting module, a multi-scale frequency-aware attention module, and a query adaptive reweighting module with bounded learnable scales. These modules are designed to enhance the representation of weak-texture and lowcontrast features while improving query-level localization and classification. The model was evaluated on the TrashCan dataset and further assessed through a transfer experiment on the DeepTrash dataset. 

Results: On the TrashCan dataset, the proposed detector achieved 93.11% precision, an F1-score of 86.63%, an mAP@50 of 91.84%, and an mAP@50:95 of 69.92%. In the DeepTrash transfer experiment, the TrashCan-trained checkpoint was adapted to the one-class plastic target dataset and achieved 91.67% precision, 79.00% recall, an F1-score of 84.86%, an mAP@50 of 86.23%, and an mAP@50:95 of 55.06%. Compared with representative baselines, including YOLOv11n, YOLO26n, YOLO26s, and RT-DETR, the improved RF-DETR achieved the strongest overall performance in the reported TrashCan evaluation and DeepTrash transfer experiment. Ablation results further showed that the three proposed modules performed best when used in combination. Discussion: The results demonstrate that the proposed frequency-aware feature enhancement and adaptive query reweighting strategies improve the detection of small and low-contrast underwater debris. The transfer results also indicate promising cross-dataset generalization. Overall, the proposed method provides a favorable trade-off between detection performance and model complexity on the evaluated datasets.

## 1 Introduction 

Marine plastic pollution has become a long-standing concern in marine conservation and ocean governance as plastic production and consumption continue to rise. Plastic waste is now documented throughout the water column, from near-surface layers to the seafloor, and its presence is associated with habitat damage, coral stress, and wider ecological disturbance. As a result, marine monitoring is increasingly built around sensor networks, automated interpretation, and robot-assisted surveys rather than manual inspection alone. Work on integrated observing systems, seafloor litter surveys, AI-based pollution monitoring, deep-sea autonomous observation, and underwater robotic autonomy reflects this operational change (Maximenko et al., 2019; Madricardo et al., 2020; Prakash and Zielinski, 2025; Barnard et al., 2024; Rohan et al., 2025). 

Underwater plastic debris detection also differs markedly from litter detection on land because the visual evidence is less stable and the scene composition is far more variable. The target set spans recognizable objects such as bottles, cans, bags, and cups, but also films, fragments, ropes, and partly hidden residues whose outlines are incomplete or deformed. In this paper, two datasets are used, each serving a different purpose. The ablation experiments and part of the comparative experiments are conducted on the 22-class TrashCan dataset (Hong et al., 2020), while the transfer experiment is conducted on the one-class DeepTrash dataset (Tata et al., 2021). Survey papers repeatedly return to the same unresolved issues: impaired image quality, small targets, domain shift across scenes, and deployment constraints (Er et al., 2023; Jian et al., 2024). A detector intended for marine-debris monitoring therefore needs to remain reliable across both relatively clear views and scenes affected by clutter, low contrast, and changing acquisition conditions. 

Performance typically deteriorates once attenuation, scattering, color cast, and non-uniform lighting erase fine contours and weaken object–background contrast. For that reason, many underwater detectors couple recognition with compensation for image degradation. MarineDetNet inserts image enhancement into the pipeline (Lakshmanan et al., 2025). Multi-distortion modeling likewise shows that degradation handling can change downstream detection quality (Liu et al., 2026), and methods combining enhancement with deformable convolution further blur the boundary between restoration and feature extraction (Guo et al., 2025). Other designs pursue the same goal through stronger feature extraction, fusion, and attention-based heads (Li et al., 2025a, b). 

Many recent systems still favor YOLO-style formulations because they are easy to deploy and computationally efficient. LFN-YOLO, DyAqua-YOLO, and CEH-YOLO pursue that direction through lightweight reparameterization, dynamic adaptation, and composite enhancement (Liu et al., 2025; Li and Peng, 2025; Feng and Jin, 2024). For marine debris specifically, instance segmentation, UTNet, and improved YOLO variants show that streamlined architectures can still perform well in difficult scenes (Corrigan et al., 2023; Cui et al., 2025; Deluxni and Sudhakaran, 2025; Yuanwei et al., 2026). Yet the present task is not primarily defined by real-time operation or maximum frame rate. In deep-sea monitoring, where data transmission can be difficult and repeated acquisition is limited, the more 

important requirement is a reliable prediction from each available image rather than a decision accumulated through many rapid detections. 

This priority becomes sharper in deep-sea inspection, fixedpoint robotic grasping, and low-frequency sampling. These operating modes involve costly deployment, limited communication bandwidth, sparse opportunities for reacquisition, and little room for manual verification after the fact (Barnard et al., 2024; Rohan et al., 2025). When a target appears briefly or from a narrow viewpoint, useful progress means reducing misses and false alarms in each captured frame rather than only accelerating inference. Models that exploit broad scene context and allow task-specific decoder behavior are therefore appealing. 

For this reason, using Transformer-based models is a promising choice. A series of existing studies has shown that DETR variants, such as UW-DETR and Efficient-Deformable-DETR, also achieve strong performance in underwater detection (Li et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2026). At the same time, a large body of research indicates that relying solely on local appearance is insufficient to effectively distinguish target objects from the surrounding background. In such cases, a global attention mechanism with a broader receptive field and stronger contextual connectivity can enable better performance in underwater detection, which is also the motivation for introducing the query adaptive reweighting module in this paper (Liu et al., 2022; Meng et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022). 

Among DETR-type models, RF-DETR is selected as the baseline for its searched feature pathway, end-to-end set prediction, and query-based global reasoning rather than for real-time inference as the core objective (Robinson et al., 2026). This design better matches the deep-sea monitoring setting emphasized in this paper: when data transmission is difficult and repeated acquisition is limited, each available image should support a reliable decision instead of relying on many rapid detections. Its real-time capability is therefore considered a secondary inherited advantage, while the main motivation is stable single-image prediction with a favorable balance between representation quality and model complexity. Compared with UWDETR, Efficient-Deformable-DETR, and RTDETR, RF-DETR provides a more general searched detector scaffold for inserting the proposed frequency-domain, multi-scale, and query-side modifications. Such a scaffold is valuable for underwater plastic debris detection, where target size varies widely, morphology is unstable, and sources of background interference are diverse. At the same time, recent studies highlight the importance offrequencyaware representation in degraded visual scenes. Underwater frequency refinement, frequency channel attention, Fourier-domain modeling, global filtering, high-frequency multispectral attention, and multi-scale frequency fusion all suggest that frequency information can improve feature discrimination when texture is weak and background energy is dominant (Wang et al., 2025; Qin et al., 2021; Li et al., 2021; Rao et al., 2021; Ying et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024). These observations motivate the integration of Fourier-domain band reweighting, multi-scale feature refinement, and query-level recalibration into the RF-DETR framework.

RF-DETR is selected as the baseline because the unmodified model already combines a searched feature pathway, end-to-end set prediction, NMS-free Transformer decoding, and global relationship modeling. These characteristics are useful for reliable single-image decisions in deep-sea debris monitoring: global context helps distinguish weak debris cues from visually similar backgrounds, set prediction reduces duplicate detections in clutter, query-level object reasoning is suitable for small or irregular targets, and the searched architecture provides a favorable accuracy–complexity trade-off when each transmitted frame is important. Therefore, RF-DETR is a structurally competitive detector for the baseline experiment itself. Its real-time capability is retained as an auxiliary deployment benefit, whereas the baseline selection is driven by reliability-oriented representation and decoding properties. 

Based on this motivation, this paper develops Improved RFDETR for underwater plastic debris detection. The model introduces an underwater frequency-aware feature reweighting module, a multi-scale frequency-aware attention module, and a query adaptive reweighting module, thereby strengthening feature modeling and query decoding under low-contrast, weak-texture, and cluttered conditions. 

The main contributions of this paper are as follows: 

1. We construct the Improved RF-DETR framework for underwater plastic debris detection. While preserving the end-to-end detection paradigm, the model performs coordinated optimization at the stages of feature modeling and query decoding. 

2. We propose an underwater frequency-aware feature reweighting mechanism and a multi-scale frequency-aware attention mechanism. The former shifts the Fourier spectrum to the center, applies centered low-/high-frequency radial masks, and then reconstructs the feature by inverse shifting and inverse Fourier transform, whereas the latter fuses parallel multi-scale convolutions, forms a stable spatial low-/high-frequency approximation, and refines the encoder input through optional gating, dropout, and residual scaling. 

3. We design a query adaptive reweighting strategy that learns a bounded scale for each base query, reuses the same basequery scales across grouped queries during training, and strengthens query response and matching stability for small, weak-texture, and easily confused targets during decoding. 

4. We conduct systematic experiments on underwater debris datasets. Comparative experiments, ablation studies, and transfer validation support the effectiveness and practical relevance of the proposed method. 

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews common object detection models, as well as related work on underwater object detection and small-object detection. Section 3 introduces the architecture and improvements of the improved RFDETR in detail. Section 4 describes the experimental configurations and settings. Section 5 focuses on the experimental results and analysis of observed phenomena. Section 6 concludes the paper and discusses future research directions.

## 2 Related work 

### 2.1 General object detection frameworks 

Recent underwater detectors increasingly borrow from DETRstyle formulations instead of relying only on convolutional dense predictors. UW-DETR adapts RT-DETR with feature fusion for underwater imagery (Li et al., 2024); Efficient-Deformable-DETR uses deformable attention to keep computation manageable while improving small-target handling (Xie et al., 2025); and improved RT-DETR variants optimize feature fusion and feature extraction while reducing computational complexity and model size to some extent (Lin et al., 2026). What makes these models relevant here is the way global relational modeling compensates for unreliable local texture and irregular boundaries. 

In real underwater scenes, many types of marine debris are occluded by other objects or mixed with surrounding materials. As a result, the model cannot reliably identify the correct targets based only on local regions. A large body of research has shown that incorporating surrounding background context into the decision process helps resolve ambiguous cues (Gao et al., 2024). For example, Liu’s DAB-DETR combines queries with dynamic anchor boxes (Liu et al., 2022); Meng’s Conditional DETR accelerates convergence by using conditional query constraints (Meng et al., 2021); and Wang’s Anchor DETR optimizes localization performance through query initialization and spatial priors (Wang et al., 2022). 

After comparing Transformer detector candidates, this paper adopts RF-DETR as the baseline model (Robinson et al., 2026). UW-DETR and improved RT-DETR are valuable underwater or efficient variants, but their original designs are already tied to specific fusion or efficiency strategies; Efficient-DeformableDETR mainly emphasizes deformable attention and computation control. RF-DETR instead offers a more general searched feature pathway with NMS-free set prediction and query reasoning, which is better suited to evaluating whether the proposed modules improve reliable single-image prediction under limited data transmission and sparse reacquisition. Frequency-oriented studies are also relevant: underwater frequency refinement (Wang et al., 2025) and frequency channel attention (Qin et al., 2021) both support redistributing spectral information when contrast is weak. That combination makes RF-DETR a natural base for adding frequency reweighting, multi-scale refinement, and query recalibration without abandoning end-to-end prediction. 

### 2.2 Underwater object detection 

Underwater object detection must contend with imaging degradation, scale variation, background interference, and deployment constraints. Recent reviews summarize these issues from the perspectives of image quality, small-object difficulty, dataset limitations, and practical deployment (Er et al., 2023; Jian et al., 2024). 

A large body of work addresses degradation compensation before or during detection. MarineDetNet improves marine-object detection through image-quality enhancement (Lakshmanan et al., 2025). Multidistortion modeling likewise shows that explicit compensation 

for underwater degradation can materially influence downstream performance (Liu et al., 2026). Joint enhancement with deformable convolution further highlights the interaction between restoration and feature extraction (Guo et al., 2025). Other models strengthen target discrimination through enhanced feature extraction, fusion, and attention-based heads (Li et al., 2025a, b). Viewed collectively, this literature indicates that degradation handling is most effective when it is coupled with higher-level feature modeling rather than treated as a separate step. 

In terms of detection backbones, the YOLO family remains the most widely used choice in underwater detection. LFN-YOLO improves small-object recognition through lightweight reparameterization and multi-scale fusion (Liu et al., 2025). DyAqua-YOLO enhances recognition in low-contrast and cluttered scenes through a dynamic adaptive architecture (Li and Peng, 2025). CEH-YOLO improves performance in complex underwater imagery through composite enhancement (Feng and Jin, 2024). Their continued use reflects clear engineering value for embedded platforms, online inspection, and applications that demand high frame rates. 

For marine debris specifically, research has increasingly focused on irregular morphology, blurred boundaries, and confusion with the surrounding background. Corrigan et al. improve local contour description through instance segmentation (Corrigan et al., 2023). UTNet demonstrates the feasibility of real-time marine debris recognition from the standpoint of lightweight deployment (Cui et al., 2025). A YOLOv12-based debris detector and an improved YOLOv11 network reach similar conclusions: structural optimization can improve recognition in complicated underwater scenes while retaining the speed advantages of one-stage detectors (Deluxni and Sudhakaran, 2025; Yuanwei et al., 2026). Even so, existing methods still leave two gaps for complex underwater plastic debris detection. First, many of them prioritize lightweight design, real-time operation, and frame-rate advantages, whereas deep-sea monitoring often requires stable single-image prediction under limited data transmission and sparse reacquisition. Second, convolution-dominated one-stage detectors remain constrained by local receptive fields when backgrounds are large-scale, targets are irregular, and texture cues are weak. DETR-type models, especially RF-DETR, are therefore attractive alternatives when end-to-end set prediction and global relationship modeling are more critical (Robinson et al., 2026). 

### 2.3 Small-object detection 

Underwater plastic debris detection also inherits the challenges of small-object recognition. Targets such as plastic fragments, films, and ropes often occupy only a small number of pixels and are frequently accompanied by occlusion, deformation, and background interference. Under these conditions, edge, texture, and local structural cues are easily attenuated during deep semantic extraction. Prior work shows that preserving fine-grained information and coordinating features across scales are central to reliable small-object recognition. HTDet demonstrates the value of jointly modeling global semantics and local detail through a hybrid Transformer architecture (Chen et al., 2023). The autoencoding pyramid method further shows that cross-scale feature organization

Frequency-domain modeling provides an additional prior for small and weak-texture targets. FCANet reconstructs channel responses through frequency channel attention and shows that frequency information contributes positively to feature selection (Qin et al., 2021). Fourier Neural Operator highlights the expressive power of frequency space for global information representation (Li et al., 2021). Global Filter Networks emphasize the efficiency of global modeling through frequency filtering (Rao et al., 2021). In adjacent visual tasks, high-frequency multispectral attention has improved cross-domain generalization (Ying et al., 2025), while multi-scale frequency-enhanced Transformers and frequency-spatial dual-domain fusion networks show how frequency-aware refinement can preserve boundaries and details (Yan et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024). These studies provide direct motivation for the underwater frequency-aware feature reweighting module and the multi-scale frequency-aware attention module used here. 

Viewed together, prior work on small-object detection underscores the value of multi-scale representation, frequency-aware modeling, and attention-based refinement. For underwater plastic debris, however, small object difficulty is entangled with image degradation, background clutter, irregular morphology, and limited cross-scale semantics. Compared with detectors that rely mainly on local convolutional evidence or dense candidate screening, RFDETR is structurally better aligned with this setting. Its end-to-end set-prediction mechanism reduces the loss of weak targets during candidate screening, and its global modeling helps disentangle small debris from cluttered backgrounds. RF-DETR is therefore both a reasonable baseline and a suitable substrate for the frequency reweighting, multi-scale refinement, and query recalibration introduced here. 

## 3 Methods 

This section presents the detector from the overall architecture down to the module-level changes that improve its underwater behavior. The discussion begins with Improved RF-DETR pipeline and then turns to the three added components: the underwater frequency-aware feature reweighting module (UFFR), the multiscale frequency-aware attention module (MFA), and the query adaptive reweighting module (QAR). The following subsections trace these components in the order in which the network processes information—backbone feature reweighting, pre-encoder multiscale refinement, and decoder-side query recalibration—so that their roles can be understood within the end-to-end structure of the baseline detector. 

### 3.1 Overall framework 

Building on RF-DETR (Robinson et al., 2026), this work constructs an enhanced end-to-end detector for underwater debris detection in complex scenes. The baseline Transformer detector is retained, while three targeted modifications are introduced at the 

stages of Fourier-domain feature reweighting, pre-encoder multiscale enhancement, and query-adaptive reweighting. The resulting network includes a backbone feature extractor, a multi-scale feature construction and enhancement module, a Transformer encoder decoder, and a prediction head. The proposed additions are the underwater frequency-aware feature reweighting module (UFFR), the multi-scale frequency-aware attention module (MFA), and the query adaptive reweighting module (QAR). 

When RF-DETR is transferred from general natural images to underwater debris detection, three limitations become prominent. First, the backbone features do not explicitly control the redistribution of low- and high-frequency information, so target-related detail can be overwhelmed by low-frequency background content. Second, the features delivered to the encoder lack a dedicated refinement stage for small targets and cluttered underwater scenes. Third, decoder queries enter attention with identical prior importance, even though their responsiveness to real targets can differ. UFFR, MFA, and QAR are introduced at these three stages: after the backbone, before the encoder, and before decoder attention, respectively. The overall architecture is shown in Figure 1. 

Given an input image, the backbone first extracts deep visual features. UFFR then performs a two-dimensional FFT, shifts the spectrum so that low-frequency components are centered, applies centered radial low-/high-frequency reweighting, shifts the spectrum back, and reconstructs the feature by inverse FFT. The reweighted features are passed to the neck to build multi-scale representations. MFA is inserted between the neck output and the Transformer encoder. It applies parallel multi-scale convolutions, averages the branch responses, forms a stable spatial low-/high-frequency approximation through local averaging and residual decomposition, and finally refines the feature through optional gating, Dropout2d, and an optional residual connection with a learnable gain. In the decoding stage, QAR multiplies each base query by a bounded learnable scaling coefficient, applies the scales directly to a single query set during inference, and shares the same base-query scales across grouped queries during training. 

With these additions, the detector preserves the end-to-end behavior of RF-DETR while introducing coordinated refinement in three places: Fourier-domain feature balancing, pre-encoder multiscale enhancement, and query-side recalibration.

## 4 Experimental setup 

This section details the experimental protocol used to evaluate the proposed detector. It first introduces the datasets and data splits used for in-domain training and transfer fine-tuning, then defines the evaluation metrics, and finally describes the computational environment and training configuration. The aim is to keep the reported results transparent and reproducible. 

### 4.1 Datasets 

This paper uses two underwater debris datasets. TrashCanInstance serves as the source dataset for model training, ablation experiments, and the main comparative evaluation (Hong et al., 2020), whereas DeepTrash (Tata et al., 2021) is used as the target dataset for transfer fine-tuning and testing. 

TrashCan-Instance contains 7,212 annotated images spanning 22 categories. For this work, the dataset is randomly split into training, validation, and test subsets at 70%:20%:10%, yielding 5,048, 1,442, and 722 images, respectively. The random seed is fixed at 42. As a public underwater debris benchmark, TrashCan mainly covers three broad groups of targets: underwater debris, remotely operated vehicles, and benthic fauna and flora (Hong et al., 2020). Most images originate from the J-EDI dataset, and were extracted from videos collected over many years in the Sea of Japan by JAMSTEC-operated ROVs. The class-instance distribution of TrashCan is shown in Figure 5. 

The DeepTrash dataset contains 2,866 images in total, including 2,151 training images and 715 test images (Tata et al., 2021). It contains only one target class, Plastic. The images are selected from aquatic plastic-debris field videos captured at different California locations, including South Lake Tahoe, Bodega Bay, and San Francisco Bay. DeepTrash does not overlap with TrashCan in image content, and its one-class label space differs from the 22-category TrashCan setting. 

In this paper, the ablation experiments and part of the comparative experiments are conducted on TrashCan, while the transfer experiment is conducted on DeepTrash. In the transfer experiment, the proposed model keeps the hyperparameter configuration reported in Subsection 4.4 unchanged when moving from TrashCan to DeepTrash, and the weights trained on the 22-class TrashCan dataset are loaded first. Because DeepTrash contains only the Plastic class, the category prediction head is replaced with a one-class head and reinitialized before target-domain fine-tuning. The remaining comparison models follow the same configuration policy used in the comparative evaluation: their official recommended default settings are retained and the training epoch number is unified to 50.

The F1score is the weighted harmonic mean of Precision and Recall, and mAP represents the mean Average Precision across all categories. GFLOPs is a commonly used indicator for measuring computational complexity, and the number of parameters indicates model size. ### 4.3 Experimental parameters 

All experiments were conducted on an ASUS TUF Gaming 5 Pro laptop running Windows 11. The system was equipped with an Intel Core i9-14900HX processor at 2.2 GHz, 48 GB of system memory, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4060 Laptop GPU with 8 GB of video memory. Training was implemented in PyTorch 2.5.1 +cu121, and the CUDA driver version was 12.9. The detector was built on the DETR-based PyTorch implementation released by Roboflow. To reduce fluctuations caused by random initialization and data partitioning, the random seed was fixed at 42 in all formal experiments except during the Optuna search stage. 

### 4.4 Training strategy and hyperparameter settings 

Training uses the AdamW optimizer together with a learningrate schedule that combines warmup and cosine annealing. During the initial iterations, the learning rate is gradually increased from a small value to the preset initial value in order to stabilize early optimization. It is then progressively decayed by cosine annealing. Automatic mixed precision (AMP) is enabled to reduce GPU memory usage and improve training efficiency, and gradient clipping is applied to suppress abnormal updates.

Rather than relying only on manually selected settings, this paper uses the Optuna framework with Bayesian optimization to search for suitable hyperparameters automatically. The search is performed on TrashCan with the TPE sampler and the median pruner for 50 trials, using validation mAP@50 as the optimization target. The search space includes input resolution, batch size, base feature dimension, learning rate, weight decay, dropout rate, gradient-clipping threshold, and the number of training epochs. The final configuration is input resolution 384×384, batch size 8, base feature dimension 12, learning rate 8.02 × 10<sup>−5</sup> , weight decay 1.63 × 10<sup>−5</sup> , dropout rate 0.25, gradient-clipping threshold 0.19, and a maximum training epoch number of 50. The 384×384 input resolution is used because it is the resolution selected by the hyperparameter search under the validation mAP@50 objective and because it provides a practical balance for the available 8 GB GPU. Compared with smaller inputs, it preserves more spatial detail for small and low-contrast debris after resizing; compared with larger inputs, it avoids excessive memory consumption, unstable batch sizing, and a disproportionate increase in training and inference cost. Therefore, this resolution is treated as a compromise among small-target detail preservation, detection accuracy, GPU memory usage, and computational efficiency rather than as an arbitrary setting. Early stopping is also enabled. When the monitored metric fails to improve by at least 0.01 for several consecutive epochs, training is terminated and the parameters corresponding to the best validation mAP@50 are retained. Unless otherwise stated, experiments involving the proposed model and its ablation variants use this configuration. 

For the network and loss configuration, the backbone is set to dinov2_windowed_small. The number of ViT backbone layers is 12, and the number of decoder layers is 2. Both num_queries and num_select are set to 300, and group_detr is set to 13. The loss function jointly optimizes classification loss, bounding-box regression loss, and GIoU loss, where cls_loss_coef, bbox_loss_coef, and giou_loss_coef are set to 1, 5, and 2, respectively, while auxiliary loss is retained. In addition, ia_bce_loss is enabled, varifocalloss is disabled, and focal_alpha is set to 0.25. In Hungarian matching, set_cost_class = 2, set_cost_bbox = 5, and set_cost_giou = 2. For the added modules, UFFR uses freq_radius=0.6 and use_learnable=True, with alpha_low and alpha_high initialized to 0.95 and 1.55, respectively. In the forward pass, UFFR applies fftshift before centered mask reweighting and ifftshift before inverse FFT. MFA uses mfascales=(1,2,3), 

mfa_freq_alpha=0.75, mfa_dropout=0.15, gate=True, residual=True, enable_vis=False, and residual gain initialization init_scale=10<sup>−3</sup> . QAR uses query_scale_init=1, scale_min=0.5, scale_max=2, scale_lr_factor=1, and group_detr=13; during training, the same base-query scales are shared across the 13 grouped query sets, whereas inference uses a single query group with N = 300. These settings remain aligned with the RF-DETR training paradigm while better matching the present task, which requires both classification stability and accurate localization.

## 6 Discussion 

This study focuses on reliable underwater plastic debris detection in scenes where image degradation, weak texture, cluttered backgrounds, and irregular target shapes make local appearance cues unstable. The proposed Improved RF-DETR is designed around this requirement 

rather than around minimum model size alone. By combining underwater frequency-aware feature reweighting, multi-scale frequency-aware attention, and query adaptive reweighting, the model strengthens degraded visual cues, improves feature discrimination, and adjusts query responses for more stable classification and localization. The experimental results support the effectiveness of this combined design and indicate that the added modules contribute complementary improvements rather than simply increasing model capacity. 

From an application perspective, the main value of the method is its balance between detection quality and computational cost. Underwater monitoring, deep-sea inspection, and robot-assisted surveys often rely on limited image acquisition opportunities, so a missed or unreliable single-frame prediction can be more costly than a moderate increase in computation. In this context, Improved RFDETR is not intended to replace extremely lightweight detectors in scenarios where maximum frame rate or deployment on very small devices is the only priority. Instead, it provides a stronger option for settings where single-image reliability, reduced false alarms, and accurate localization are more important. The results also show that difficult cases remain: thin, deformable, partly occluded, or background-like targets are still harder to localize tightly, and some categories with very high scores have small test subsets, meaning that their apparent performance should be interpreted cautiously.

First, the evaluation is still based on a limited number of underwater debris datasets, and the DeepTrash experiment involves transfer finetuning rather than zero-shot generalization. More diverse underwater sites, imaging devices, turbidity levels, lighting conditions, and debris distributions are needed before the robustness of the 

method can be fully established. Second, the present study mainly evaluates static images, while practical underwater systems often collect sequential observations. Temporal consistency, multi-frame fusion, and video-based uncertainty reduction may help reduce missed detections and duplicate predictions. Future research will address these limitations in several directions. In addition, future work will expand cross-domain testing to more underwater environments, investigate stronger adaptation strategies, incorporate temporal information from underwater video, and improve boundary-aware localization for thin or deformable debris.
